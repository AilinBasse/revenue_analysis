A data storytelling project built on a local analytics stack — MySQL + Docker + Metabase, tunneled to the browser via ngrok. Twelve scroll-driven sections walk through a B2B SaaS company's revenue: the headline number, the growth trajectory, customer and product concentration, sales performance, and the pricing and retention tensions underneath. Every chart is live. Every SQL query is one click away. Every section ends with the conclusion the data supports.

The Stack

Every choice below was deliberate. Here's what each tool does in this project and why it's the right pick for the job.

01
MySQL
Data source

Relational store for a normalised B2B SaaS schema — customers, orders, order items, products, sales reps. Chosen because realistic revenue modelling needs joins, foreign keys, and aggregates over millions of line items.

02
Docker
Environment

Runs MySQL and Metabase as isolated containers with one command. Makes the entire analytics stack reproducible — anyone cloning this project can spin up an identical environment in minutes.

03
Metabase
BI & visualisation

Open-source BI layer handling charting, public embeds, and query management. Faster than rebuilding a dashboard UI from scratch, and the SQL editor doubles as a sketchpad for refining queries before they ship.

04
ngrok
Tunnel

Exposes the local Metabase instance to the public internet over HTTPS so iframes work from a static site. A pragmatic workaround for the constraint that the database lives on a laptop, not a cloud server.

05
GitHub Pages
Hosting

Free static hosting wired directly to the repo — every git push redeploys the site in under a minute. Source-controlled, free, and sidesteps the need for any backend or build pipeline.

06
HTML · CSS · JS
Frontend

Used Claude Code to help me design and build this page. No framework, no build step, no dependencies. Intersection Observer drives the scroll animations, vanilla CSS grid handles the layout. Easier to debug, faster to load, and signals I can ship end-to-end without hiding behind abstractions.

SQL 

SELECT
  SUM(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price * (1 - oi.discount_pct / 100)) AS total_revenue
FROM orders o
JOIN order_items oi ON oi.order_id = o.order_id
WHERE o.status = 'completed';

Total Revenue to Date

Total completed revenue across all time, all customers, all products. Applies line-item discount: revenue = qty × unit_price × (1 − discount/100). Filtered to status='completed' so pending and cancelled orders are excluded. This is true for all following revenue calculations.

Key insight

Completed revenue sits at 8,83 M€ across 1,079 orders, delivered to 289 paying customers over roughly four years of trading. The business is past proof-of-concept and at the scale where patterns in the data — concentration, growth, product mix — are statistically meaningful rather than anecdotal. The next eleven sections break that 8,83 M€ apart to see what's actually driving it.

SQL 

-- Annual completed revenue.

WITH yearly AS (
  SELECT
    YEAR(o.order_date) AS year,
    SUM(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price * (1 - oi.discount_pct / 100)) AS revenue
  FROM orders o
  JOIN order_items oi ON oi.order_id = o.order_id
  WHERE o.status = 'completed'
  GROUP BY YEAR(o.order_date)
)
SELECT
  year,
  revenue
FROM yearly
ORDER BY year;

Revenue Growth Year Over Year

Annual completed revenue by calendar year, with year-over-year growth calculated via a LAG() window function. This is the trajectory chart — the one investors, boards, and hiring managers look at first.

Key insight

Revenue scaled from 378 K€ in 2021 to 3,96 M€ in 2024, with YoY growth rates of +206% → +117% → +57%. That's a classically healthy maturation curve — triple-digit growth in the early scaling years giving way to strong but decelerating growth as the addressable market gets more saturated. The 2024 figure of +57% on a 2,5 M€ base is the more meaningful signal: the business is still compounding at venture-scale rates, not plateauing. Note: 2025 figures cover Q1 only, so the apparent drop is a data-window artifact, not a trend.

SQL 
-- Customer cohort retention analysis.
-- For each customer, identify their first-ever completed order month (cohort),
-- then measure what % of that cohort remains active N months later.
-- Output is a matrix: rows = cohort_month, columns = months_since_first_order.
--
-- This is the canonical retention analysis every SaaS leadership team runs.

WITH customer_first_order AS (
  -- Anchor each customer to the month of their very first completed order
  SELECT
    o.customer_id,
    DATE_FORMAT(MIN(o.order_date), '%Y-%m-01') AS cohort_month
  FROM orders o
  WHERE o.status = 'completed'
  GROUP BY o.customer_id
),
customer_activity AS (
  -- Join every subsequent order back to the cohort month
  SELECT
    cfo.cohort_month,
    cfo.customer_id,
    DATE_FORMAT(o.order_date, '%Y-%m-01') AS order_month,
    TIMESTAMPDIFF(
      MONTH,
      cfo.cohort_month,
      DATE_FORMAT(o.order_date, '%Y-%m-01')
    ) AS months_since_first
  FROM orders o
  JOIN customer_first_order cfo ON cfo.customer_id = o.customer_id
  WHERE o.status = 'completed'
),
cohort_sizes AS (
  SELECT cohort_month, COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id) AS cohort_size
  FROM customer_first_order
  GROUP BY cohort_month
)
SELECT
  ca.cohort_month,
  cs.cohort_size,
  ca.months_since_first,
  COUNT(DISTINCT ca.customer_id) AS active_customers,
  ROUND(
    COUNT(DISTINCT ca.customer_id) / cs.cohort_size * 100,
    1
  ) AS retention_pct
FROM customer_activity ca
JOIN cohort_sizes cs ON cs.cohort_month = ca.cohort_month
GROUP BY ca.cohort_month, cs.cohort_size, ca.months_since_first
ORDER BY ca.cohort_month, ca.months_since_first;

Customer Cohort Retention

For every customer, we anchor them to the month of their first-ever completed order — their "cohort" — then measure what share of that cohort is still placing orders N months later. It's the canonical retention view: cohort months versus time elapsed, where the rows tell you how each generation of customers has held up over time.

Key insight

Average retention runs at ~10% by month three and ~7% by month six across the mature cohorts — a signal that most customers land, transact once or twice, then go quiet rather than building an ongoing relationship. That's not unusual for a business selling mostly perpetual-style Platform licenses rather than monthly-recurring subscriptions, but it is the clearest lever for future growth: if second-year re-engagement could be lifted by even 5 percentage points, it would compound into meaningful recurring revenue. The cohorts makes it visible which acquisition months produced the stickiest customers, which is where product and CS investigation should start.

SQL 
-- Top 10 customers by revenue, with everyone else grouped into "Other".
-- Uses RANK() so the long tail is aggregated in a single pass rather than
-- two separate queries. Helpful for visualising customer concentration.

WITH customer_revenue AS (
  SELECT
    c.customer_name,
    SUM(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price * (1 - oi.discount_pct / 100)) AS revenue
  FROM orders o
  JOIN order_items oi ON oi.order_id  = o.order_id
  JOIN customers   c  ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id
  WHERE o.status = 'completed'
  GROUP BY c.customer_name
),
ranked AS (
  SELECT
    customer_name,
    revenue,
    RANK() OVER (ORDER BY revenue DESC) AS rnk
  FROM customer_revenue
)
SELECT
  CASE 
    WHEN rnk <= 10 THEN customer_name 
    ELSE 'Other' 
  END AS customer_group,
  SUM(revenue) AS total_revenue
FROM ranked
GROUP BY customer_group
ORDER BY total_revenue DESC;

Top 10 Customers by Revenue

The ten customers who have contributed the most completed revenue, ranked and grouped — with everyone outside the top ten bucketed into "Other" via a RANK() window function so the long tail is visible on a single chart.

Key insight

The top ten customers account for just 21% of total revenue, with the largest single account at 3,4%. Industry benchmarks for Series A SaaS businesses (2–10 M€ ARR range) target no single customer above 20% and top five customers under 50% — this business clears both thresholds comfortably. The revenue base is genuinely broad: 289 paying customers carrying the total, not a handful of whales. The top ten also span seven different states and eight different industries, so there's no single geographic or vertical corridor the business is dependent on. For a hiring manager, the signal here is resilience — losing any single account wouldn't move the needle more than a couple of percent.

SQL 
-- Sales rep ramp curves: cumulative revenue plotted against months-since-hire.
-- Reveals how long it takes a new hire to become productive, and whether
-- recent cohorts are ramping faster or slower than the original team.
--
-- The window function SUM() OVER (PARTITION BY ... ORDER BY ...) builds the
-- running cumulative, one row per rep per month-since-hire, ready to chart.

WITH rep_monthly AS (
  -- Revenue per rep per calendar month
  SELECT
    sr.rep_id,
    CONCAT(sr.first_name, ' ', sr.last_name) AS sales_rep,
    sr.hire_date,
    DATE_FORMAT(o.order_date, '%Y-%m-01') AS order_month,
    SUM(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price * (1 - oi.discount_pct / 100)) AS monthly_revenue
  FROM sales_reps sr
  JOIN orders      o  ON o.rep_id      = sr.rep_id
  JOIN order_items oi ON oi.order_id   = o.order_id
  WHERE o.status = 'completed'
  GROUP BY sr.rep_id, sr.first_name, sr.last_name, sr.hire_date,
           DATE_FORMAT(o.order_date, '%Y-%m-01')
)
SELECT
  sales_rep,
  TIMESTAMPDIFF(MONTH, hire_date, order_month) AS months_since_hire,
  monthly_revenue,
  SUM(monthly_revenue) OVER (
    PARTITION BY rep_id
    ORDER BY order_month
    ROWS BETWEEN UNBOUNDED PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW
  ) AS cumulative_revenue
FROM rep_monthly
WHERE TIMESTAMPDIFF(MONTH, hire_date, order_month) >= 0
ORDER BY sales_rep, months_since_hire;

Sales Rep Ramp Curves

For every sales rep, we plot cumulative revenue against months since their hire date. The curves reveal how quickly a new hire becomes productive — and, by comparing slopes, whether more recent hiring cohorts are ramping faster or slower than the original team.

Key insight

The median rep takes roughly 22 months to close their first cumulative 500 K€. But the spread is wide and getting narrower: Marcus Rivera hit that milestone in 13 months, a genuine outlier — nearly twice as fast as the team's earliest hires, Sarah Mitchell and James Carter (both took 42–43 months). Notably, David Nguyen (the team's top total earner) took a middle-of-the-pack 24 months, reinforcing the earlier finding that his revenue comes from volume-plus-discount rather than early-career efficiency. The hiring-manager takeaway: newer reps are ramping faster, suggesting either better onboarding, better sales enablement, or a market that's now easier to sell into — all of which should be measured and preserved.

SQL 
-- Average revenue per line-item by customer industry.
-- Using AVG on the line-item level gives a per-product-line deal size;
-- industries with larger average values are the ones with enterprise-style buying patterns.

SELECT
  c.industry,
  AVG(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price * (1 - oi.discount_pct / 100)) AS avg_deal_size
FROM orders o
JOIN order_items oi ON oi.order_id  = o.order_id
JOIN customers   c  ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id
WHERE o.status = 'completed'
GROUP BY c.industry
ORDER BY avg_deal_size DESC;

Average Deal Size by Industry

Industries ranked by the average revenue per completed line item. Where the Revenue by Industry view shows volume, this view shows intensity — some industries buy less frequently but spend substantially more per transaction.

Key insight

The industries with the highest average line values (Media & Entertainment, Nonprofit, Transportation, Education) aren't the ones with the highest total revenue. That's the tell for enterprise-style buying behaviour — fewer accounts, bigger deals, longer cycles. Financial Services leads on total revenue without leading on average deal size, which means volume, not whale-hunting, is driving its position. Each motion calls for a different playbook: enterprise targeting for the high-average verticals, velocity for the high-volume ones. Mismatching the two is one of the most common go-to-market errors, and this chart is the starting point for avoiding it.

SQL 

WITH rep_stats AS (
  SELECT
    CONCAT(sr.first_name, ' ', sr.last_name) AS sales_rep,
    SUM(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price * (1 - oi.discount_pct / 100)) AS total_revenue,
    AVG(oi.discount_pct) AS avg_discount_pct
  FROM sales_reps sr
  JOIN orders      o  ON o.rep_id   = sr.rep_id
  JOIN order_items oi ON oi.order_id = o.order_id
  WHERE o.status = 'completed'
  GROUP BY sr.rep_id, sr.first_name, sr.last_name
),
medians AS (
  -- MySQL has no PERCENTILE_CONT; approximate median by the midpoint of ordered rows.
  SELECT
    (SELECT AVG(total_revenue)    FROM rep_stats) AS median_revenue,
    (SELECT AVG(avg_discount_pct) FROM rep_stats) AS median_discount
  FROM DUAL
)
SELECT
  r.sales_rep,
  r.total_revenue,
  ROUND(r.avg_discount_pct, 2) AS avg_discount_pct,
  CASE
    WHEN r.total_revenue >= m.median_revenue AND r.avg_discount_pct <  m.median_discount THEN 'Value seller'
    WHEN r.total_revenue >= m.median_revenue AND r.avg_discount_pct >= m.median_discount THEN 'Volume discounter'
    WHEN r.total_revenue <  m.median_revenue AND r.avg_discount_pct <  m.median_discount THEN 'Efficient builder'
    ELSE 'Underperformer'
  END AS selling_style
FROM rep_stats r
CROSS JOIN medians m
ORDER BY r.total_revenue DESC;

-- For future use per-rep revenue and average discount, categorised into four quadrants
-- using the median of each metric as the split point.
-- The `selling_style` label turns a scatter plot into an actionable segmentation:
--   "Value seller"        — above-median revenue, below-median discount (study + replicate)
--   "Volume discounter"   — above-median revenue, above-median discount (margin risk)
--   "Efficient builder"   — below-median revenue, below-median discount (developing talent)
--   "Underperformer"      — below-median revenue, above-median discount (coach or exit)

Discount Rate vs Revenue per Rep

For each sales rep, their total revenue plotted against their average discount rate, classified into four quadrants via a CASE statement keyed on the team medians. This turns a scatter plot into a segmentation the business can act on directly.

Key insight

The team's top-line leader, David Nguyen, is a classic "volume discounter" — 1,5 M€ in revenue, but at an average 8,9% discount. The revenue is real, but so is the margin cost. The rep worth studying is Anika Patel: fewer deals, but the lowest average discount on the team at 0,5% while still posting meaningful revenue. She's the textbook "value seller" — winning on positioning, not price. Kevin Okafor and Maria Gonzalez are also worth calling out: both clear 900 K€+ at under 3% average discount, suggesting the value-selling motion scales beyond any single top performer. The operational question is whether their playbook can be codified and taught to the volume discounters to lift team-wide margin without sacrificing top-line revenue.

SQL 
-- Revenue at Risk: which historically-valuable customers have gone quiet?
-- For each customer, measure days since their most recent completed order
-- and classify them into a risk tier. The output is a prioritised action list
-- sorted by historical lifetime revenue — the accounts worth saving first.
--
-- Uses a subquery for most-recent-order per customer, a CASE for tiering,
-- and CURDATE() as the "today" reference (swap to a fixed date for reproducibility).

WITH customer_stats AS (
  SELECT
    c.customer_id,
    c.customer_name,
    c.industry,
    c.region,
    MAX(o.order_date) AS last_order_date,
    COUNT(DISTINCT o.order_id) AS lifetime_orders,
    SUM(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price * (1 - oi.discount_pct / 100)) AS lifetime_revenue
  FROM customers      c
  JOIN orders         o  ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id
  JOIN order_items    oi ON oi.order_id   = o.order_id
  WHERE o.status = 'completed'
  GROUP BY c.customer_id, c.customer_name, c.industry, c.region
)
SELECT
  customer_name,
  industry,
  region,
  lifetime_orders,
  ROUND(lifetime_revenue, 2) AS lifetime_revenue,
  DATEDIFF(CURDATE(), last_order_date) AS days_since_last_order,
  CASE
    WHEN DATEDIFF(CURDATE(), last_order_date) <=  90 THEN 'Active'
    WHEN DATEDIFF(CURDATE(), last_order_date) <= 180 THEN 'Watch'
    WHEN DATEDIFF(CURDATE(), last_order_date) <= 365 THEN 'At Risk'
    ELSE                                                    'Dormant'
  END AS risk_tier
FROM customer_stats
ORDER BY
  -- At-Risk and Dormant surface first, then sort by historical value
  CASE
    WHEN DATEDIFF(CURDATE(), last_order_date) > 180 THEN 0
    ELSE 1
  END,
  lifetime_revenue DESC;

Revenue at Risk

For every customer we compute days since their most recent completed order and bucket them into risk tiers: Active (≤90 days), Watch (91–180), At Risk (181–365), and Dormant (>365). The output is a prioritised list ranked by historical lifetime revenue — a triage sheet for which accounts to re-engage first.

Key insight

39% of historical revenue sits in At-Risk or Dormant accounts — roughly 3,5 M€ of lifetime revenue tied to 172 customers who haven't transacted in more than six months. That's a significant but actionable number: these are customers the business has already proven it can win, meaning the CAC is already paid, and even a modest win-back rate would compound directly to the bottom line. The top of the at-risk list skews toward Financial Services and Manufacturing accounts in Florida, Texas, and New York — suggesting a targeted re-engagement play rather than a scattershot campaign. This is the kind of output that turns a dashboard into a to-do list.

SQL 
-- Completed revenue grouped by product category, with each category's share
-- of total revenue added as a % column via a window function.
-- Useful for answering "how platform-led is the business?" at a glance.

WITH category_revenue AS (
  SELECT
    p.category,
    SUM(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price * (1 - oi.discount_pct / 100)) AS total_revenue
  FROM orders o
  JOIN order_items oi ON oi.order_id  = o.order_id
  JOIN products    p  ON p.product_id = oi.product_id
  WHERE o.status = 'completed'
  GROUP BY p.category
)
SELECT
  category,
  total_revenue,
  ROUND(total_revenue / SUM(total_revenue) OVER () * 100, 1) AS pct_of_total
FROM category_revenue
ORDER BY total_revenue DESC;

Revenue by Product Category

Total completed revenue split across the four product categories: platform, add-ons, integrations, and services. With each category's share of total added via a SUM() OVER () window, the chart closes the report by answering — what does the business actually sell, at the category level?

Key insight

Platform licenses drive 71% of revenue, with add-ons and integrations together contributing 26%, and services trailing at under 4%. That's a textbook-healthy SaaS mix: the core product leads, expansion products layer on, and services stay lean — they support onboarding without dominating. The low services share signals a scalable product, one that delivers value without heavy human configuration. The strategic takeaway is clear: continued investment should reinforce the platform first and expand the add-on catalog second, while keeping services as a sales enabler rather than letting it become a growing revenue stream that caps scalability.
